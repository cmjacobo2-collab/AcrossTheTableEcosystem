Who this is for

You teach. Online courses, corporate training, certification programs, coaching cohorts, workshops. Your product is knowledge arranged so somebody else can use it.

This guide is for the course creator or training business deciding how much of the production to automate, and where automating it would quietly destroy what people are paying for.

That tension runs through the whole guide. It is worth sitting with.

Start with the work, not with the apps

These are not twenty lists of AI apps. Each stack starts with the jobs a particular business performs, then matches AI and conventional software to those jobs. Treat it as a menu to evaluate, not a shopping list.

For this kind of business the work runs in one line, start to finish: Student → Enrollment → Learning → Assessment → Retention

Broken into the steps a customer actually moves through:

Prospect → Enrollment → Course → AI-assisted learning → Assessment → Completion → Next program

Read that line left to right. Somebody is interested, they enroll, they learn, they are assessed, and they either complete and come back for the next thing or they drift away.

Completion is the number that matters and almost nobody manages it. Low completion means low word of mouth, low repeat purchase and refund requests. The money in education is in the second program, which only sells to people who finished the first.

What to watch out for in each one

None of these are reasons not to buy. They are the things owners tell us they wish they had known first.

Thinkific — Marketing is still on you.
Teachable — Transaction fees on lower plans.
Zoom — Recording consent, again.
ChatGPT — It sounds confident when it’s wrong. Check anything that touches money, law or a customer.
Claude — Same rule as any assistant: verify facts and never paste data you wouldn’t email.
Canva — Easy to drift off-brand. Lock a brand kit.
HeyGen — Disclose synthetic presenters. Get consent for any real likeness.
Synthesia — Heavier process, heavier price.
ElevenLabs — Voice cloning needs explicit consent.
HubSpot — Costs step up sharply by tier and contact count.
Stripe — Per-transaction fees. Watch disputes.

Where AI actually fits in this business

Curriculum development, quizzes, student support, content personalization, course summaries, instructional media.

In plain terms, that means:

Building the curriculum outline and the assessment questions from material you already have.
Producing the course workbook, the summaries and the handouts that otherwise never get finished.
Answering routine student questions so instructor time goes to the hard ones.
Translating or re-recording a module for a different audience without filming again.
Reading where students drop out and telling you which module is losing them.

Here is the uncomfortable question for this industry. If your course is information that a student could now ask a chatbot for directly, the course is under pressure. What survives is what AI cannot give them: a cohort, accountability, feedback on their own work, a credential that means something, and your judgment about their specific situation. Build toward those.

What to set up first

Do these in order. Finish one before you start the next. Most owners who try to do all of it in one month end up using none of it.

  1. Pick one course platform and keep enrollment simple. Every extra step between interest and enrollment costs you students.
  2. Measure completion by module. Before improving anything, find out where people stop. It is almost never where you expect.
  3. Fix the drop-off point. Usually that means breaking up a module, adding a checkpoint, or replacing a long video with something a person can do.
  4. Add support that is not you. Routine questions answered automatically, hard ones escalated. This is what lets a course scale past your own calendar.
  5. Then add production tools. AI video, narration and translation open up new audiences. They are leverage on a course that already works, not a way to fix one that does not.

What it costs to start

Course platforms charge a monthly fee, and some also take a percentage of sales. At volume the percentage model costs far more than it looks like it will — do the arithmetic at your expected revenue, not your current one.

Payment processing is a percentage of every enrollment.

Video and voice production tools are per month or by minutes generated. These are the ones to add last, because high production value does not rescue a course with a content or structure problem.

The price column in the table above tells you how each one charges — per user, per location, per contact, or a percentage of every sale — not what you will pay. That is deliberate. The charging model is what decides whether a tool stays affordable as you grow, and it is the part that does not change every quarter. Get the current dollar figure from the vendor’s own pricing page before you budget.

The rules underneath all of it

Legal · Privacy · Data protection · Security · Compliance · Records · Audit · Risk · Human oversight — underneath every one of the twenty stacks, not an afterthought.

If you issue certifications or credentials, understand what you are claiming they mean. Misrepresenting accreditation is a serious matter.

Student records, particularly for minors or in formal education settings, carry privacy obligations that are stricter than ordinary customer data.

Disclose AI-generated instructors or narration. A synthetic presenter that students believe is a real teacher is a trust problem you cannot recover from.

You need the rights to everything in your course — images, music, clips, quotes. AI-generated material is not automatically free of claims.

Refund terms must be clear and honored. Education has high refund expectations and consumer protection rules frequently apply.

Mistakes that cost the most

Measuring enrollments and ignoring completions.
Producing hours of video because it feels like value, when shorter and more practical teaches better.
Using an AI presenter without telling students.
Selling information a chatbot can supply, instead of the feedback and accountability it cannot.
Choosing a platform on revenue share without modeling what that costs at scale.

The CEO decision

What is your completion rate, by module?
What are students actually buying — the information, or the structure and feedback around it?
How many students buy a second program from you?
What percentage of your revenue does your platform take, and at what volume does that get painful?
If a student asked an AI assistant your course’s core question, what would still be missing?

Your quick review

☐ I know completion rate by module.
☐ Enrollment takes as few steps as possible.
☐ Routine student questions are handled without me.
☐ Any AI-generated instructor or narration is disclosed.
☐ I have rights to every asset in the course.
