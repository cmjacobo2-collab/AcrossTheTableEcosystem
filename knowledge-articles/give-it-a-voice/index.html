The job these three do

Some things are better heard than read. A phone greeting, a training module, a walkthrough of how something works.

Recording them yourself means a quiet room, several takes, and editing. So the greeting stays robotic and the training stays a PDF nobody opens.

These three turn written words into spoken ones. They are built for genuinely different jobs, and one of them is not really for publishing at all.

Before you clone anyone’s voice

Copying a real person’s voice — yours, a colleague’s, anyone’s — needs their explicit permission, in writing. These tools make it easy. That does not make it yours to do.

Who each one is

ElevenLabs — the realistic one. Realistic AI voice generation and cloning in many languages. Best for Narration, IVR, audio versions of written content. Watch out: Voice cloning needs explicit consent.

ElevenLabs produces the most convincing voices, in many languages, and can copy a specific voice. That last ability is why the permission rule above matters.

Murf — the narration one. Studio-style AI voiceover with timing controls. Best for Training and explainer narration. Watch out: Fewer voices than ElevenLabs.

Murf is built for business narration with timing controls, so the voice lines up with what is on screen. Fewer voices, more control over delivery.

Speechify — the listening one. Text to speech for listening and light narration. Best for Turning documents into audio you can consume. Watch out: Not built for production voiceover.

Speechify is for consuming rather than producing — turning documents into audio you listen to while driving. Not built for finished voiceover work, and the article is clear about that.

How a business actually uses each one

A business with a phone system. A proper greeting and menu, recorded once from a script, re-recorded in two minutes when the hours change. Compare that with booking a studio or using whoever in the office sounds best.

A company producing training. Murf narrates the modules with timing matched to the slides. When a procedure changes, one line of script changes and the audio is regenerated.

An owner with no reading time. Speechify turns long documents into audio for the drive. Nothing is published. The value is entirely in getting through material you would otherwise not read.

What it costs

ElevenLabs and Speechify have free tiers. Murf is paid. ElevenLabs charges by how much audio you generate, which suits occasional use well.

For a one-off job like a phone greeting, the free tier of ElevenLabs will almost certainly cover it. Do not subscribe for something you will do twice a year.

Where this sits in your stack

An AI voice becomes much more useful when you build production around it. Start with your script. ElevenLabs, Murf or Speechify handles the appropriate audio job. Descript can help edit recorded content. HeyGen can pair voice with an AI presenter when that’s appropriate. Canva creates the supporting visuals. Then the finished material can go to YouTube for marketing or into your learning-management system for employee or customer training. One script can become multiple business assets.

Descript — audio and video editing
HeyGen — AI presenter
Canva — visual production
YouTube — distribution
Learning management system — training distribution

The CEO decision

Is this for customers to hear, or for you to listen to? Different tools entirely.
Do you need more than one language? That is where generated voice clearly beats recording yourself.
If you clone a voice, do you have written permission from that person?
How often will you actually use it? Twice a year means free tier, not subscription.

Apply it to your business

Listen to your own phone greeting as a customer would. Then read one paragraph of your most important written material and ask whether anyone has ever read it.

If the greeting is poor or the material goes unread, you have a use. If both are fine, this is a tool looking for a problem and you can skip it.
