Capture Meetings: Everything Agreed Out Loud, Written Down Without Taking Notes

Three weeks later the customer remembers it differently and you cannot prove otherwise. A notetaker fixes that — if you ask permission every time.

About 5 min read

Download Knowledge Brief (PDF) .pdf · 106 KB

Illustrative figures are not actual client results.

The business problem

You have a good call with a customer. You agree the price, the start date, and the one unusual thing they want doing. You remember all of it for about four days.

Three weeks later they say you agreed something different. Maybe they are right. You genuinely cannot remember, because you were driving, or on site, or it was one of nine calls that day.

You either give in and lose the money, or you argue and damage the relationship. Both outcomes trace back to the same thing: nobody wrote it down.

What business owners need to understand

There are tools that sit in your calls, listen, and afterwards hand you a written record. You get a full transcript, a short summary, and a list of what everyone agreed to do. You do not take notes. It arrives in your email a few minutes after you hang up.

The three most used are Fireflies, Otter and Fathom. They each have a free version with a monthly limit, which is plenty to find out whether you want it.

The one rule you must not break

You have to tell people they are being recorded, every single time, before it starts.

This is not a courtesy. In some states it is the law that everyone on the call has to agree, and Arizona businesses regularly deal with customers in states where that applies. The tools make an announcement, but you should say it yourself as well.

One sentence at the start: “I use a notetaker on my calls so I don’t miss anything — is that all right with you?” Nobody has ever said no to that, and it is the sentence that keeps you safe. If anyone objects, turn it off and take notes by hand.

How to apply it

1. Pick one and connect your calendar. Fireflies, Otter or Fathom. Free version. It needs to see your calendar so it knows which meetings to join.

2. Decide which calls it joins. Customer and supplier calls, yes. Private conversations and anything personal, no. Set this up before your first call, not after.

3. Say the sentence, every time. Then wait for the answer before you continue.

4. Read the summary straight after. Five minutes while it is fresh. Fix anything it got wrong — names and prices are where it slips most.

5. Send the agreed points to the customer. This is the part that makes it worth money. Now you both have the same record, in writing, that day.

6. Delete what you do not need. Decide how long you keep recordings — ninety days suits most — and stick to it.

An example

A kitchen fitter quoted a job over the phone and agreed to include a particular tap at no extra cost. He forgot entirely. The customer remembered. That one argument cost him a $280 tap and a relationship worth about $4,000 a year in referrals.

Now every quoting call is recorded with permission, and within an hour the customer gets a short email: here is what we agreed, including the tap. Disputes stopped, and customers started treating him as more professional than his competitors, purely because something arrived in writing the same day.

Work through the numbers

Count the disagreements about what was agreed over a year. Six, at an average of $250 given back, is $1,500. Add the ninety minutes a week spent writing up notes — roughly 78 hours a year. The tool costs nothing on the free plan, or about $18 a month.

Most owners underestimate the first line badly, because each disagreement feels like a one-off. Count last year properly before you decide this is not worth it.

What the financial statements reveal

This hides inside other numbers rather than having a line of its own. Money given back in disputes usually lands in discounts, credits or write-offs, where it never gets examined.

Look at your credits and allowances over the last year. Ask how many were real goodwill and how many were you losing an argument you had no record of. There is a smaller effect on cash too: a customer who gets written confirmation the same day queries the invoice less, and an invoice that is not queried gets paid sooner.

The CEO decision

Do your agreements actually happen on calls? Will you say the permission sentence every time — because if you will not, do not use these tools at all. Will you read the summaries? How long will you keep recordings, and where?

Apply it to your business

Think back over the last twelve months and count the times a customer remembered an agreement differently from you. Write down what each one cost. If the total is under a few hundred dollars, leave this one alone. If you flinched while adding it up, you have found the job worth starting with.

Your quick review

☐ How many disagreements about what was agreed did I have last year?
☐ What did they cost me in total?
☐ Which calls will the notetaker join, and which will it never join?
☐ What exactly will I say at the start of every call?
☐ Who gets the written summary afterwards — just me, or the customer too?
☐ How long do I keep recordings, and who else can see them?
