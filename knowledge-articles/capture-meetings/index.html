The job these three do

All three sit in your calls, listen, and afterwards hand you a written record. You get a transcript of what was said, a short summary, and usually a list of what everyone agreed to do. You take no notes. You do nothing during the call. It arrives in your email a few minutes after you hang up.

They differ in what happens to that record afterwards, and that is the only question that matters when you are choosing.

Read this before you use any of them

You must tell people they are being recorded, every time, before it starts. In some states the law requires everyone on the call to agree, and your customers will not all be in Arizona.

Say it out loud: “I use a notetaker so I don’t miss anything — is that all right with you?” If anyone objects, switch it off and take notes by hand. This is not a courtesy; it is the sentence that keeps you safe.

Who each one is

Fireflies — the one that feeds your other tools. Records, transcribes and summarises meetings, then pushes those notes into your other systems. Best for teams who keep losing decisions made on calls. The point of Fireflies is not the transcript — it is that what was said ends up in your customer system and your task list without anyone copying it across. Watch out: tell people they are being recorded, and set a rule for how long you keep recordings.

Otter — the one that is best while you are still talking. Live transcription and summaries, strong in the moment. Best for note-taking while you are still in the conversation. Pick this if what you want is an accurate record of what was said, rather than a machine that does something with it afterwards. Watch out: transcript quality drops when people talk over each other, and with strong accents.

Fathom — the one with the least admin. A meeting recorder with fast, clean summaries and customer-system sync. Best for sales calls where the follow-up matters more than the transcript. It has the most generous free version of the three, which makes it the easiest to try without a decision attached. Watch out: fewer connections to other software than the bigger platforms.

Choose Fireflies if your meetings contain valuable sales or operations information. Choose Otter if you mainly need reliable notes. Choose Fathom if you want fast summaries after the call and little else.

The question to answer first: if you are missing follow-ups and decisions after meetings, do not start by asking which product has the most features. Start by deciding what information you need after every meeting. That answer picks the tool for you.

How a business actually uses each one

Fireflies in a trades business. A quoting call happens. Afterwards the customer’s details and what they asked for land in the customer system automatically, the three things promised become tasks with dates, and the owner sends the customer a short written summary the same day. The dispute about what was agreed never happens, because there is a record and the customer already has it.

Otter in a consulting practice. A long client session is recorded and transcribed. The consultant wants the actual words, because the value is in what the client said and how they said it, not a summary of it. The transcript gets read properly afterwards and the useful phrases go into the proposal.

Fathom in a sales role. Six calls in a day. Nobody has time to read six transcripts. What is needed is six short summaries and a list of who to chase, in the inbox by five o’clock. Fathom does that and does not ask for anything else.

What it costs

All three have a free version with a monthly limit on meetings, enough to find out whether you want it. Paid plans are per person per month and sit in a similar range. Fathom’s free tier is the most generous, so if you are simply trying the idea out, start there.

Work through the numbers

Count two things. How often did a customer remember an agreement differently from you last year, and what did each cost in money given back or time arguing? And how long do you spend writing up notes after calls?

Six disagreements at $250 each is $1,500 a year. Ninety minutes a week on notes is about 78 hours. The tool costs nothing on the free plan, or roughly $18 to $30 a month. Most owners underestimate the first number badly, because each disagreement feels like a one-off.

What the financial statements reveal

This hides inside other numbers rather than having a line of its own. Money given back in disputes usually lands in discounts, credits or write-offs, where nobody examines it.

Look at your credits and allowances over the last year and ask how many were genuine goodwill and how many were you losing an argument you had no record of. There is a cash effect too: a customer who gets written confirmation the same day queries the invoice less, and an invoice that is not queried gets paid sooner.

Where this sits in your stack

Recording the meeting is only step one. The tool makes the summary — then important customer information goes into your customer system, action items become tasks, and your team gets the summary wherever they already talk.

Now the meeting does not end when everybody hangs up. The conversation becomes customer information, assignments and follow-up work. That is the question this series keeps asking: the AI captured something — what happens to that information next?

The CEO decision

What information do you need after every meeting? Answer that before comparing features. Will you say the permission sentence every time — because if not, do not use these tools at all. Will anyone read the summaries? How long do you keep recordings, and who can see them?

Apply it to your business

Count the times over the last twelve months that a customer remembered an agreement differently from you, and what each cost. If the total is under a few hundred dollars, leave this alone. If you flinched while adding it up, start with whichever of the three matches the information you actually need afterwards.

Your quick review

☐ What information do I need after every meeting?
☐ How many disagreements about what was agreed did I have last year, and what did they cost?
☐ Which calls will the notetaker join, and which will it never join?
☐ What exactly will I say at the start of every call?
☐ Who gets the written summary — just me, or the customer too?
☐ How long do I keep recordings, and who else can see them?
