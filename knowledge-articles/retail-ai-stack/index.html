Who this is for

You sell products. Maybe from a shop, maybe from a website, most likely both. You carry inventory, which means your money is sitting on shelves, and your biggest decisions are what to buy and what to mark down.

This guide is for the owner who started in one channel and added the other, and now maintains two sets of product information, two sets of stock counts and two versions of the truth.

The goal here is one business with two front doors, not two businesses pretending to be one.

Start with the work, not with the apps

These are not twenty lists of AI apps. Each stack starts with the jobs a particular business performs, then matches AI and conventional software to those jobs. Treat it as a menu to evaluate, not a shopping list.

For this kind of business the work runs in one line, start to finish: Customer → Inventory → Sale → Retention

Broken into the steps a customer actually moves through:

Inventory → Store or website → Customer → Sale → Customer profile → Marketing → Repeat purchase

Read that line left to right. You buy stock, a customer finds you in the shop or online, they buy, and you either know who they were afterwards or you do not.

The single most expensive mistake in retail technology is letting in-person and online stock live in two systems. It leads to selling something you do not have, which costs you the sale and the customer, and to holding stock you think you already sold.

None of these are reasons not to buy. They are the things owners tell us they wish they had known first.

Square — Per-transaction fees. Watch chargebacks.
Klaviyo — Priced by profile count.
Gorgias — Ecommerce-shaped. Poor fit for services.
QuickBooks — Only as clean as your categorization habits.
Canva — Easy to drift off-brand. Lock a brand kit.

Where AI actually fits in this business

Product descriptions, merchandising ideas, customer segmentation, support, marketing personalization, inventory analysis.

In plain terms, that means:

Writing product descriptions for a hundred items in an afternoon, which is the job that otherwise never gets finished.
Grouping your customers by what they actually buy, so the email about hiking boots goes to the people who buy hiking boots.
Drafting answers to the same twelve order questions you get every week, so support stops eating your mornings.
Reading last season’s sales and telling you which lines earned their shelf space and which ones quietly did not.
Turning one product photo shoot into the website copy, the email and the social posts without rewriting it three times.

AI will not fix a buying problem. If the stock is wrong, better product descriptions sell the wrong stock slightly faster. Use it on the merchandising analysis before you use it on the marketing.

What to set up first

Do these in order. Finish one before you start the next. Most owners who try to do all of it in one month end up using none of it.

  1. Make one system the truth for inventory. Whatever you choose, in-person and online sales must draw down the same stock count. Until that is true, everything else is built on numbers you cannot trust.
  2. Connect sales to accounting. Daily totals should land in the books without anyone typing them. This also makes your margins visible, which is the number retail runs on.
  3. Start collecting customer details at the till. Email or phone, with permission. A retail business without a customer list is renting its customers from whoever sent them.
  4. Then add marketing. Once you have a list and know what people bought, segmented campaigns are the cheapest revenue in retail. Before that, you are emailing strangers.
  5. Add a support desk when the questions outgrow the inbox. Not before. A shared inbox is fine until it is not, and you will know.

What it costs to start

Retail pricing has two shapes and you will pay both. Platform fees are per month, which is predictable. Payment processing is a percentage of every sale, which is not — it grows exactly as fast as you do.

Because of that percentage, the processing rate is the most financially significant number in your stack. A fraction of a percent, on a year of sales, is often larger than everything else on this list combined. Compare it on your real volume.

Email and SMS marketing tends to be priced by how many contacts you keep. That makes list hygiene a cost control, not just good manners — you are paying every month for people who have not opened anything in two years.

The price column in the table above tells you how each one charges — per user, per location, per contact, or a percentage of every sale — not what you will pay. That is deliberate. The charging model is what decides whether a tool stays affordable as you grow, and it is the part that does not change every quarter. Get the current dollar figure from the vendor’s own pricing page before you budget.

The rules underneath all of it

Legal · Privacy · Data protection · Security · Compliance · Records · Audit · Risk · Human oversight — underneath every one of the twenty stacks, not an afterthought.

Card data is your processor’s responsibility, not yours, which is a reason to use a real payment system and never to record card numbers anywhere yourself.

Email and SMS marketing is regulated. You need permission to send, a working unsubscribe, and for text messages the rules are stricter than for email. Collect consent properly at the till and online.

Customer purchase history is personal data. Know where it lives and who can export it, especially if staff turn over.

If you sell across state or national borders, sales tax and consumer-protection rules follow the customer, not your shop. Your platform can help with this; it cannot decide it for you.

Mistakes that cost the most

Running separate stock counts for the shop and the website, then selling what you do not have.
Choosing a platform on monthly fee and ignoring the processing rate, which is the bigger number.
Building a large email list you pay for every month and never clean.
Discounting to move stock without checking whether the line was ever profitable.
Collecting customer emails and then never sending anything, which is the worst of both worlds.

The CEO decision

Do in-person and online sales reduce the same inventory number? Yes or no.
What percentage do you pay on card sales, and when did you last compare it?
What is your gross margin by product line, and can you see it without building a spreadsheet?
How many of last year’s customers could you email tomorrow?
Which lines are you carrying out of habit rather than evidence?

Your quick review

☐ One inventory count covers every channel I sell through.
☐ Sales post to accounting automatically.
☐ I collect customer contact details with permission at the point of sale.
☐ I know my processing rate and my margin by line.
☐ My email list is cleaned on a schedule.
