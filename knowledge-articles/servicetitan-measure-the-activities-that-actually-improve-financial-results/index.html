ServiceTitan: Measure the Activities That Actually Improve Financial Results

A dashboard should drive decisions. Learn to measure whether a new habit, like estimate follow-up, actually raises your close rate and profit.

About 3 min read

Download Knowledge Brief (Word) .docx · 490 KB

Educational example | Financial figures are hypothetical unless otherwise noted.

The strategy: what the business owner should understand

The purpose of a dashboard is to help make decisions. The business should measure whether a new behavior leads to better conversions and contribution, not just how often a tool is opened.

A contractor introduces estimate follow-up reminders. Management wants to know whether the reminders justify the subscription.

How to apply the strategy

Capture lead quality, quote response time, conversion, completed jobs, direct costs and customer satisfaction. Compare similar periods and pilot before scaling.

Before acting, write down the baseline, the owner of the process, the cost of the change, and the evidence that would make you continue or stop.

Build the financial model before making the decision

Close rate = booked jobs ÷ qualified estimates. Incremental contribution = extra completed jobs × (revenue/job − variable cost/job). Net EBITDA change = incremental contribution − incremental overhead.

100 qualified estimates × 30% = 30 jobs. After pilot, 36% = 36 jobs. Six jobs × $700 = $4,200 extra revenue. Six × $420 = $2,520 extra direct costs. The tool and follow-up effort cost $350/month. EBITDA improvement = $4,200 − $2,520 − $350 = $1,330.

A percentage-point change is the difference between two percentages. For example, moving from 10% to 12% is a two-percentage-point improvement. Do not call it a 2% revenue increase.

Where to find the evidence in your statements

Profit and loss (P&L)

Find revenue, cost of sales, operating expenses, depreciation, interest and taxes. Confirm that added costs and savings are coded to the same categories before comparing periods.

Balance sheet

Review cash, accounts receivable (A/R), inventory, accounts payable (A/P) and any new borrowing. Higher revenue can produce higher A/R before it produces cash.

Cash flow and bank activity

Separate actual payments from noncash entries. Depreciation reduces accounting profit but is not a current-period cash payment. Payroll, inventory and software payments may happen before customers pay.

Operational reports

Use the metric closest to the strategy: defects, access exceptions, follow-up rates, invoices processed, handoff errors, product conversions, status calls, pilot orders or completed jobs.

How one decision affects the rest of the business

A higher close rate may create a scheduling backlog; customer complaints and refund costs can offset gains.

Sales and marketing: check whether more demand requires more leads, follow-up time or customer support. Operations: verify capacity, quality and turnaround. Accounting: track revenue recognition, direct costs, overhead and receivables. Cash: forecast the timing of supplier payments, payroll and customer collections.

How the owner should decide

Require evidence of a sustained conversion lift, completed jobs and adequate capacity. Check whether the customer mix or pricing changed before crediting the tool.

Use a 30-day pilot when feasible. Define the expected change, measurement owner, cost ceiling, quality safeguards and stop condition before starting.

The lesson to take away

A corporate strategy becomes useful to a small business only when the owner can explain the problem, calculate the expected benefit, identify which other functions will be affected, and verify the result in both operational and financial records.
