The job these three do

All three of these are assistants you talk to by typing. You ask a question or describe a problem in your own words, and they answer. You can argue with them, ask them to try again, or tell them to explain it more simply.

They overlap a great deal. Any of the three will draft an email, summarise a document or help you think through a decision. That is why choosing between them confuses people — on the surface they look identical.

They are not identical. Each one is built around a different main job, and once you know what that job is the choice becomes obvious.

Who each one is

ChatGPT — the general one. Made by a company called OpenAI. The one most people have heard of, and the one most businesses end up using as their everyday assistant. It handles writing, analysis, research and, with its Agent mode, multi-step work it carries out for you. Best as your default thinking partner when you want one tool that does most jobs well. Watch out: it sounds confident when it is wrong, so check anything that touches money, law or a customer.

Claude — the document one. Made by a company called Anthropic. Less famous, and the one people move to once they are working with longer material. Strong on long documents, careful reasoning and drafting in your voice. Best for reading contracts, policies and long reports without losing the thread. Watch out: same rule as any assistant — verify facts, and never paste data you would not put in an email.

Perplexity — the research one. This works differently from the other two. It is an answer engine: it searches the live internet and shows you where each part of its answer came from. Best for market checks, vendor research and “what changed this quarter” questions. Watch out: sources can be thin, so click through before you quote a number to anybody.

Note what that table does not say. None of them is harder to use than the others, and all three suit a small business. The difference is what each is built to do, not how advanced it is.

Which one to open first: if you are completely new to AI, start with a broad general-purpose assistant. Then add specialised tools once you discover the business jobs where you need more capability. Do not sign up for all three and compare — that is a way of not starting.

How a business actually uses each one

A week with ChatGPT. Monday you describe an awkward customer situation and ask what you might be missing. Tuesday you paste rough notes and ask for a tidy quote email. Thursday you describe a job you are hiring for and ask it to draft the advert. Friday you ask it to explain a term your accountant used that you did not want to admit you did not know.

A week with Claude. A supplier sends a fourteen-page contract and you ask what obligations it puts on you. Three years of policy documents need to become one plain-language staff handbook. A long report arrives and you need the four things in it that affect you.

A week with Perplexity. You are quoting a job in a town you do not usually work in and need the going rate. A customer mentions a regulation you have not heard of. You are choosing between two suppliers and want to know what people actually say about them.

What it costs

All three have a genuinely usable free version. You do not need to pay anything to find out whether this helps you.

Paid plans are priced per person per month and sit in a similar range to each other. Use the free version for a month first. Most small businesses find one assistant on the free tier covers what they need, and the ones who outgrow it know exactly why by then.

The one thing all three get wrong

They can be confidently wrong. Not hesitant, not vague — wrong in a clear, well-written, entirely believable sentence.

This matters most on numbers, dates, legal points and anything about a specific company. Treat an answer as a well-read friend’s opinion, not a professional’s advice. Anything touching money or law gets checked before you act on it. And keep customer names, bank details and anything you would not want repeated out of all three.

Where this sits in your stack

An assistant on its own is useful. Connected to the rest of your business it is considerably more useful.

Your company information lives somewhere — Google Drive or a knowledge system like Notion. Your assistant helps analyse that information and create the strategy or content. Canva turns those ideas into visual assets. Zapier automates what happens next, sending information between your business applications.

So the assistant is not operating by itself. It is connected to the information, the creative tools and the workflows that actually run your company. That is the difference between a clever toy and part of how the business works.

The CEO decision

Which job do you have most of — everyday writing and thinking, long documents, or research? That answers which to start with. Will you use it weekly? Are you clear that it can be wrong? Do you know what you will never paste into it?

Apply it to your business

Pick the one that matches your most common job and use only that one for two weeks. Keep a note each time it saved you something and each time it got something wrong. At the end of two weeks you will know whether this is worth paying for, and which of the three you actually needed — a better answer than any comparison article can give you.

Your quick review

☐ Which of the three matches the job I have most of?
☐ Have I used the free version for a fortnight before paying for anything?
☐ What will I never paste into it?
☐ Which answers do I check before acting on them?
☐ Where does my company information live, so the assistant can be pointed at it?
☐ What happens to what it produces — does anyone do anything with it?
